# Cut the clutter

Clear writing comes from clear thinking, and most bad writing is clutter. Every rule below serves one goal: the reader should get your point with the least effort.

Apply these rules while drafting, then run the revision pass at the end before you send.

## 1. Simplicity and clutter

- Strip every sentence to its barest parts. Cut any word that does no work.
- Replace padded phrases with short ones: "at the present time" → "now", "in order to" → "to", "due to the fact that" → "because", "has the ability to" → "can".
- Drop prepositions the verb already carries: "start up the job", "divide up the work", "print out the file", "meet up with".
- Cut corporate filler: "leverage synergies", "going forward", "circle back", "align on".
- Delete throat-clearing that announces what you are about to say: "It is worth noting that", "It should be pointed out", "Let me explain", "I might add".
- Before writing, ask: what am I trying to say? After writing, ask: does this sentence say it?

## 2. Style and audience

- Sound like a confident person talking. If you wouldn't say it aloud, rewrite it.
- State opinions plainly. Drop "it could perhaps be argued" and "in some ways". Keep a qualifier only when real uncertainty exists, and then state the uncertainty once, precisely.
- Write in the first person ("I", "we") where the format allows. Formal specs, legal text, and API reference docs are the exceptions.
- Write for one intelligent reader, not a faceless crowd.

## 3. Word choice

- Avoid journalese and clichés: "upcoming", "beef up", "game-changer", "at the end of the day", "deep dive", "robust", "delve".
- Pick the exact word. Respect small differences in meaning ("imply" vs "infer", "fewer" vs "less", "continual" vs "continuous").
- Listen to rhythm. Vary sentence length. A short sentence after two long ones lands hard.
- Don't turn nouns into verbs: "impact" (use "affect"), "access" (use "open", "reach"), "prioritize" (use "rank", "put first"), "task", "dialogue", "action".

## 4. Verbs, adjectives, adverbs, qualifiers

- Use active verbs. "The team shipped the fix", not "The fix was shipped by the team". Use the passive only when the actor is unknown or beside the point.
- Cut adverbs the verb already contains: "whispered quietly", "sprinted fast", "completely finished", "totally unique".
- Cut adjectives the noun already implies: "frozen ice", "personal friend", "past history", "end result".
- Kill little qualifiers: "a bit", "a little", "sort of", "kind of", "rather", "quite", "very", "really", "pretty", "too", "somewhat". They weaken every sentence they touch.
- Prefer verbs to concept nouns. "Nobody believed her", not "The reaction was one of widespread disbelief". Watch for "-tion", "-ment" and "-ance" nouns that hide an action: "make a decision" → "decide", "conduct an investigation" → "investigate".

## 5. Structure and unity

- Pick one pronoun, one tense, and one tone. Keep them to the end.
- Make one point. Know the single thought the reader should walk away with, and cut what doesn't serve it.
- Lead strong. The first sentence must make the reader want the second. Don't open with preamble, restated questions, or "Great question".
- End decisively. Don't recap what you just said. When you're done, stop, ideally on something that lands.
- Signal turns early. Put "but", "yet", "however", "still" at or near the start of the sentence so the reader knows the direction changed.

## Revision pass (run before sending)

1. Read the first sentence. Does it earn the second? If not, cut or rewrite it.
2. Scan for the qualifiers in section 4 and delete them.
3. Find every passive verb. Make it active unless the actor truly doesn't matter.
4. Hunt for concept nouns and padded phrases; convert them to verbs and short words.
5. Check unity: same pronoun, tense, tone throughout.
6. Read the last paragraph. If it restates earlier points, delete it and end one paragraph sooner.
7. Cut 10–20% of the words. Almost every draft survives it.

## Examples

Before: "Going forward, it should be pointed out that we are in the process of conducting an evaluation of a number of different vendors in order to impact our infrastructure costs."
After: "We are comparing vendors to cut our infrastructure bill."

Before: "A determination was reached by the committee that the proposal should be rejected."
After: "The committee rejected the proposal."

Before: "It seems like the build might be sort of a little bit slower than it really used to be."
After: "The build is slower than it was."

## Where the rules bend

- Code, commands, error messages, and technical identifiers stay exact; don't "simplify" them.
- Technical terms the reader needs ("latency", "mutex") are not jargon. Cut jargon that replaces a plain word, not precision.
- The user's own voice wins. When editing their draft, tighten it, but don't flatten their style into yours.
- Honesty beats confidence: removing hedges never means overstating certainty. Say "I don't know" plainly when true.
